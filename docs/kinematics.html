1Six joints, six angles

The reference frame has its origin on the shoulder axis, which crosses the base rotation axis. z points up. With every angle at zero the arm stands straight up; a positive shoulder angle leans it towards +y. In the robot, each joint shows its axis as a dashed line.

q2, q3 and q5 rotate about x (they bend); q1, q4 and q6 rotate about z (they spin). Shoulder and elbow are parallel with no sideways offsets, which makes everything else much simpler.

2Forward kinematics: the chain

Each joint is a rotation and each bone a translation along z. Multiplying the matrices from left to right walks from the base to the fingers: each factor starts from the frame left by the previous one.

T = Rz(q1)·Rx(−q2)·Tz(Lu)·Rx(−q3)·Rz(q4)·Tz(Lf)·Rx(q5)·Rz(q6)·Tz(d6) ⎡1 0 0 ⎤ ⎡c −s 0⎤ Rx = ⎢0 c −s ⎥ Rz = ⎢s c 0⎥ c = cos θ, s = sin θ ⎣0 s c ⎦ ⎣0 0 1⎦

3The spherical wrist trick

The pronation (q4), pitch (q5) and roll (q6) axes all pass through one point: the wrist centre, highlighted in the robot. Turning about any of these axes does not move that point. So its position depends only on q1, q2 and q3:

r = Lu·sin q2 + Lf·sin(q2+q3) distance from the base axis z = Lu·cos q2 + Lf·cos(q2+q3) height above the shoulder p_wrist = ( −r·sin q1 , r·cos q1 , z ) now: r = z = p_wrist =

This splits the problem into two problems with three unknowns each: the first three joints bring the wrist to the right point, the last three orient the hand. Industrial six-axis arms use the same scheme (it is called Pieper decoupling).

4Inverse, part one: where the wrist goes

Start from the desired grip point p and the finger direction a (the hand z axis). The wrist sits d6 further back along a:

q3 comes from the law of cosines on the shoulder, elbow, wrist triangle (in the robot: Lu, Lf and d). There are two solutions, positive and negative q3: elbow up or elbow down. A third choice is to turn the base by 180° and lean backwards. That makes four ways to bring the wrist to the same point.

If d is more than 435 mm the point is out of reach and the cosine leaves [−1, 1]: the firmware must reject the command before moving any motor.

5Inverse, part two: orienting the hand

With q1, q2 and q3 known, the forearm orientation before pronation, R03, is known too. What is missing to reach the desired orientation R is the wrist rotation:

The sign in front of the square root gives two solutions: wrist up or down, with pronation and roll turned by 180°. Times the four of step 4, that makes eight solutions. In "Inverse" mode you see them all under the sliders; the ones outside the limits are struck through.

The endless roll simplifies one thing: q6 has no limits, so the firmware always picks the turn closest to the current angle. The hand never has to unwind.

6Singularities: where the formulas jam

In three situations two joints do the same thing and the robot loses a degree of freedom. Near these poses, small grip moves ask the joints for huge rotations. The three indicators at the top of the robot show how close you are.

  • Straight wrist (q5 = 0). Pronation and roll turn about the same axis: only q4 + q6 counts. The firmware keeps the pronation still and puts everything on the roll, which is endless and so does not mind.
  • Straight arm (q3 = 0). The wrist is at the edge of the workspace (435 mm from the shoulder): it cannot go further, and elbow up and elbow down coincide.
  • Wrist above the base (r = 0). The wrist centre is on the yaw axis: q1 can be anything. This is the most annoying one, to be avoided in trajectories.

The rest pose, all zero, has all three at once. It is fine for parking, but trajectories should start by bending the elbow.

7From joints to motors

Base and shoulder have one motor each. Elbow and wrist are differentials: two motors drive two side bevels, and the satellite between them makes the second rotation. Turning together, they bend; turning in opposite directions, they spin.

elbow (5:1 gearbox, belts 160/20: 40; bevels 30/15 → kp = 0.5) A = 40 · (−q3 + q4/2) B = 40 · (−q3 − q4/2) wrist (belts 78/16 = 4.875; bevels 1:1) A = 4.875 · (q5 + q6) B = 4.875 · (q5 − q6) and back q3 = −(A+B) / (2·40) q4 = (A−B) / 40 q5 = (A+B) / (2·4.875) q6 = (A−B) / (2·4.875)

Rotor = motor shaft angle in the same direction as the part it moves; steps with 200 × 16 microsteps per turn. The real direction of each motor is set once in the firmware (DIR pin) during homing. The sign of the base and of the cycloid depends on that direction.

The encoders read the joints directly, not the motors: AS5600 on the elbow shaft (q3), on the idler satellite (−q4), on the pitch axle (q5) and in the T fitting (q6). Closed-loop control therefore works on joint angles, without undoing the differential. For the roll, the angle within one turn is enough: with endless rotation any turn will do.

8In the code

Everything on this page is in control/kinematics.py (NumPy, no other dependency). Positions are in the shoulder frame: add 183 mm for the height above the desk.

from kinematics import fk, ik, hand_frame, joints_to_steps p = [252.8, 212.1, 40 - 183] # grip 4 cm above the desk R = hand_frame(p, tilt=0, spin=0) # fingers pointing down q = ik(p, R, q_prev=q_now) # closest solution inside the limits steps = joints_to_steps(q) # 6 integers for the TMC2209s

Checks done on the model:

  • The chain matches the matrices of the 3D arm viewer within 10⁻¹³ mm on 500 random poses.
  • On 3000 random poses inside the limits, all eight inverse solutions put the grip back where it was (error below 10⁻¹² mm).
  • Starting close to the original pose, the inverse finds the same branch, except within 3° of the straight arm, where the two branches coincide.
  • The "pick the apple" pose of the animation puts the grip 40 mm above the desk with the fingers vertical.
